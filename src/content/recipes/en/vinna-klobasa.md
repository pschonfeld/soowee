directions:
  - title: Grind the meat
    steps:
      - Tear {2 white bread rolls} into small pieces, pour over {100 ml whole milk} and soak in the fridge for {chill:1h}.
      - Meanwhile rinse the salt off the {sheep casings} under running water and soak them in lukewarm water for 30 minutes. Then keep them in cold water in the fridge until stuffing; they need at least 2 hours in water in all.
      - Cut {200 g pork shoulder} and {450 g pork belly} into 2–3 cm cubes, keeping them apart and trimming off any sinew. For the last 30 minutes of the soak, put the cubes in the freezer in a single layer on a plate or in a shallow dish, together with the grinder body, auger, blade and plate. Put the food processor bowl and blade, the mixer bowl, the paddle and one extra bowl in the fridge. The cubes should be firm at the edges.
      - Grind the shoulder through the finest plate at speed 4 into the food processor bowl, then the belly into the mixer bowl. The fat should come out in distinct white specks, not smeared.
      - tip: If the fat starts to smear into a grey paste or the grinder squeaks, stop and chill everything for 15 minutes. Warm fat never binds back, and the sausage turns out crumbly and greasy.
      - Mash the soaked rolls with a fork into a smooth paste with any milk left in their bowl. Refrigerate the paste and both bowls of meat for {chill:30m}.
  - title: Mix the filling
    steps:
      - Add {20 g salt}, {0.5 tsp white pepper}, {0.5 tsp ground mace}, a pinch of {ground nutmeg} if you like, {0.25 tsp ground cloves} and the finely grated zest of {0.5 lemon} to the shoulder in the food processor and pulse in 2–3 second bursts for {timer:20-30s}, until it turns into a sticky paste.
      - With the motor running, pour in {100 ml whipping cream}, {80 ml dry white wine} and {1 egg} a little at a time, and stop as soon as the paste is smooth and glossy, after about 45 seconds. It should stay below 10 °C; if it is warmer, chill it for 15 minutes.
      - tip: Salt draws the proteins out of the lean meat first, and the liquids are then worked into that sticky base instead of loosening it. This is how a butcher builds the bind in a bowl cutter.
      - Scrape the paste onto the belly in the mixer bowl, add the bread paste and mix with the paddle at speed 2 for {timer:1-2m}, until the filling is fine, binding and stretches in threads when pulled apart.
      - Fry a walnut-sized patty in a dry nonstick pan for {timer:3-4m}, until cooked through, and taste it. For a more Christmassy note, add up to {0.25 tsp ground cloves} and mix for 30 seconds more.
      - Refrigerate the filling for {chill:1h}. Put the grinder body, auger and stuffer tube back in the freezer at the same time.
      - tip: Cloves quickly turn medicinal, so add the rest only if the patty asks for it. The salt is already a full measure, about 1.9 %; add more only if the patty is clearly under-seasoned.
  - title: Stuff the sausage
    steps:
      - Assemble the cold grinder without the blade and plate, with the small stuffer tube, and wet the tube. Slide the soaked casing onto the tube, leaving about 10 cm hanging free.
      - Fill the grinder with the filling and run it at a low speed, feeding it down with the pusher, until the filling reaches the tip, then knot the casing. Find the speed on the first metre; it should be slow enough for you to keep up and not trap air.
      - Fill the casing in one long rope, steadying it with one hand as it comes off the tube. Fill it full but soft, so that a gentle pinch leaves a dent.
      - tip: An overfilled casing bursts when it is coiled and when it meets heat. If the auger warms the filling, stop and chill it for 15 minutes. A vertical piston stuffer is worth buying if you will make sausages regularly; it fills without warming or trapping air.
      - Prick any air pockets with a clean pin. Twist the rope into {8} sausages of about 30 cm, cut through the twists and knot the ends.
      - Lay the sausages on a rack, or on a large plate or tray lined with baking paper or a clean tea towel, uncovered, and keep them in the fridge until cooking.
      - tip: Cook the sausage within 24 hours of mixing the filling, because the milk and wine in it start to ferment and sour.
  - title: Cook the sausage
    heat: Preheat the oven to **160 °C**, conventional top and bottom heat, and heat a wide pot of water to **80 °C** on a thermometer, barely trembling.
    steps:
      - Coil each sausage loosely into a flat spiral and push two skewers through it crosswise to hold it.
      - Lower one coil into the water for {timer:30-60s}, until the casing turns white and tight, and check that it holds. Scald the rest one or two at a time, lift them out and pat them dry.
      - tip: The scald shrinks the casing evenly before it meets dry heat, so it does not split in the oven. Hotter water makes the filling expand faster than the casing can give, and it bursts in the pot instead.
      - Lay the coils on a baking tray lined with paper, brush them on both sides with {1 tbsp pork lard} and bake for {bake:15m}.
      - Turn the coils, baste them with the fat from the tray and bake for {bake:5-10m} more, until the centre reads 64 °C. Push the probe in from the side into the middle of the coil. Switch off the oven and leave the door ajar.
      - Brown the coils in three batches in the frying pan over medium-high heat, sharing {2 tbsp pork lard} between the batches, for {timer:1-2m} on each side, until deep golden and the centre reads 70 °C. Keep the finished coils in the warm oven and let them rest for {cool:3-5m} before serving.
      - tip: The filling is raw pork, so it must reach 70 °C in the centre. Don’t go much beyond that, because the sausage dries out quickly and it should be juicy.

## Chef’s Notes

- **Make ahead:** Coil the raw, unscalded sausages and freeze them on the day you make them, each one flat in its own freezer bag; they keep 3 months. Thaw overnight in the fridge and cook within 24 hours of thawing, starting with the scald.
- **Store:** Cooked sausage keeps 2 days in the fridge. Reheat it in the oven at 160 °C, or slice it and fry it briefly in pork lard. Salt any unused casings well again and keep them in a closed container in the fridge, never in the freezer.
- **Swap:**
  - **With beef.** For a slightly leaner, darker sausage, replace 150 g of the pork belly with beef chuck.
  - **Pork casings.** You can use 28/30 mm pork casings in place of sheep casings. They’re thicker and chewier, so give the sausages a few extra minutes in the oven.
  - **In the pan.** Dust the scalded coils with flour and fry in pork lard over medium-low heat. Cover for the first 8 minutes, then fry uncovered until golden on both sides and the centre reaches 70 °C.
  - **Without casings.** Shape the filling into small patties and fry them in pork lard as you would the coils in the pan.
- **Serve:** With buttery mashed potatoes, or potato salad on Christmas Eve; otherwise with sauerkraut, rye bread and mustard.

## Allergens

- **Gluten:** bread rolls.
- **Egg:** egg.
- **Milk:** milk and cream.
- **Sulphites:** white wine.
